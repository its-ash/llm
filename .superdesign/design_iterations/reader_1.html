Understanding Attention Mechanisms

Written by Codex · drafted with an LLM, lightly edited

Every transformer model rests on one simple move: instead of reading a sentence strictly left to right, it looks at every word at once and asks, for each one, which other words in the sequence actually matter to it right now. That question, repeated across layers and positions, is what we call attention. It sounds almost too simple to explain why these models write coherent paragraphs, hold a train of thought across pages, or translate a sentence while preserving its tone — but the mechanism scales in ways that earlier architectures never could.

Mechanically, attention works by turning every token into three vectors: a query, a key, and a value. The query represents what a given token is "looking for," the key represents what each token "offers," and the value is the information actually passed along once a match is found. A token's query is compared against every other token's key using a dot product, producing a raw score for how relevant that pair is. Those scores are scaled, passed through a softmax function so they sum to one, and then used as weights to blend the value vectors together. The result is a new representation of the token that has absorbed context from everywhere it needed to.

Why "self" attention matters

In a self-attention layer, the queries, keys, and values all come from the same sequence — the sentence is, in effect, attending to itself. This is what allows a model to resolve a pronoun by looking back at the noun it refers to, or to recognize that "bank" means something different next to "river" than it does next to "loan." Stack enough of these layers together, each one refining the representation a little further, and the model builds up something close to an understanding of structure — not because it was told the rules of grammar, but because attending to context repeatedly makes certain patterns statistically unavoidable to learn.

Multi-head attention pushes this further by running several of these query-key-value computations in parallel, each with its own learned projections. One head might specialize in tracking subject-verb agreement, another in resolving coreference, another in something with no clean human name at all. Their outputs are concatenated and projected back down, giving the model a richer, higher-dimensional view of the same sentence than any single attention pattern could provide alone.

The cost of looking everywhere

The tradeoff is cost: comparing every token to every other token means the computation grows quadratically with sequence length. Doubling the context window roughly quadruples the attention workload, which is precisely why long-context models have needed clever approximations — sparse attention, sliding windows, and various forms of caching — to stay usable. Understanding this tradeoff is often the first step toward understanding why context length, latency, and cost are so tightly coupled in practice.

None of this requires the model to "understand" language in a human sense. It only requires that attending to the right context, over and over, across billions of examples, produces representations useful enough to predict the next token convincingly. That attention turns out to be enough to write essays, hold conversations, and explain itself back to you is, arguably, the most surprising empirical result in the whole field.
