Core Concept

Understanding Attention

12 min read · Generated from attention.md

Before attention mechanisms, sequence models processed text strictly left to right, compressing everything they had seen into a single fixed-size memory. That worked, until sentences got long and the model started forgetting the beginning by the time it reached the end.

Attention solves this by letting every output position look back at every input position directly, and decide — for each token it is producing — which earlier tokens matter most right now. Rather than one memory bottleneck, the model gets a dynamic, weighted view of the entire input.

“Attention lets a model ask a question of its own context, and listen only to the parts that answer it.”

Mechanically, this happens through three learned projections of each token: a Query, a Key, and a Value. The query from one position is compared against the keys of all positions to produce relevance scores, which are turned into weights and used to blend the values.

Stack this operation across many heads and many layers, and the model builds increasingly abstract relationships — from matching nearby words, to tracking long-range grammatical dependencies, to representing entire ideas.

Why It Scales So Well

Because every position can attend to every other position in parallel, attention-based architectures parallelize far better on modern hardware than the strictly sequential models that came before them. That parallelism, as much as the mechanism itself, is why this idea now underlies nearly every large language model in production.
